How Estate Sale Companies Build Referral Partnerships

Make it easy for another professional to understand your fit, explain your process, and introduce a client with confidence.

By Joel PrentissPublished November 8, 2026

In this guide

  2. Understand each partner
  3. Build a capability sheet
  4. Use a specific introduction
  5. Keep a useful follow-up record
  6. Handle the first introduction

Start with the handoff you can make easier

A professional introduction puts someone else's reputation alongside yours. Give that person enough information to judge whether your firm fits the situation, and make the first contact straightforward for the family.

For a Texas estate sale or liquidation company, this means being clear about service area, timing, scope, and communication. For a personal-property appraisal firm, it also means describing the kinds of contents and assignments you actually handle. Do not blend appraisal, liquidation, moving, and cleanout services into a single promise if different people provide them.

In this guide, a referral partnership means an ongoing professional relationship. It does not imply an exclusive arrangement, guaranteed introductions, compensation, or an agreement to exchange clients. This is a business-process guide, not legal advice.

Ask what each professional needs to know

Use these as conversation prompts, not assumptions about every practice. Let the person explain their workflow before proposing yours.

Real estate agents: sequencing and scope

Ask which dates affect photography, repairs, showings, or possession. Explain what must happen before your team can begin and what condition the property will be left in under the agreed scope. Distinguish a target date from a confirmed commitment. An agent should not have to infer whether removal or cleaning is included.

Probate and elder-law attorneys: a clear operational boundary

Ask who should receive scheduling questions, what documentation the client expects, and how communications should be handled. Describe your own process and available records accurately. Leave advice about legal authority, court requirements, and deadlines with the client's attorney. A marketing conversation is not the place to interpret estate documents.

Senior move managers: decisions and responsibilities

Ask when keep, move, and sale items will be identified, who manages changes, and which team handles each task. Discuss the point at which your staff can enter and the point at which the next team takes over. A simple responsibility list can reveal gaps before they become a rushed decision on moving day.

Senior communities: a usable resource for residents

Ask whether the community maintains an outside-service list and how providers are considered. Offer a short explanation residents can understand: who you help, where you work, what the first conversation covers, and how to reach you. Respect the community's contact preferences and the family's pace.

A local research starting point: NASMM's Texas directory lists member businesses and their stated service areas. Read the current entries for Georgetown, Round Rock, Cedar Park, Austin, and nearby communities, then verify fit directly. A directory listing is a research lead, not an endorsement or a referral relationship.

A firm based in Georgetown might begin with professionals whose work overlaps its actual Williamson and Travis County service area. Keep the list small enough that you can learn each person's process. Avoid treating a statewide directory as a mailing list.

Build a capability sheet someone can forward

One clear page is enough to start. A recipient should be able to forward it without having to explain your business on your behalf.

  • Who you help: the situations and contents your established firm is equipped to handle.
  • What you do: list each service and identify work performed by a separate provider. State exclusions that commonly affect fit.
  • Where you work: actual cities or areas, any travel considerations, and how you evaluate requests outside them.
  • How work starts: initial contact, qualification, consultation, scope discussion, and scheduling. Describe availability as current information, not a standing guarantee.
  • What can be verified: current credentials, memberships, or insurance information you can substantiate. Use the exact designation and identify who holds it.
  • What the client receives: describe your actual communication and closeout process. Share only permission-cleared, redacted examples or clearly marked blank samples.
  • Who handles the introduction: one contact, a direct route, and a response expectation your team can maintain.

Add a revision date. Recheck the page when your services, staffing, or coverage changes. If a personal-property appraisal is a separate engagement, say so; do not suggest that sale pricing is automatically a formal appraisal report.

Your website can carry the same information on a page for professionals. The website checklist explains how to make that page useful without turning it into a list of unconfirmed affiliations.

Use a specific introduction

This is an original template for an estate-service business owner to adapt. Replace the brackets with facts you can support. Remove services you do not offer and use the recipient's preferred business contact method.

Subject: A local resource for [specific service] in [area]

Hello [Name],

I'm [Name], [role] at [Company]. We help [client type] with [specific service] in [actual service area]. I noticed [a relevant, public detail about the recipient's work].

I'd like to understand where handling household contents creates delays or extra coordination for your clients. Our process starts with [your actual first step], and [contact name] handles scheduling and updates.

Would a brief conversation be useful? I can also send a one-page overview of our scope, current availability, and what clients receive. If another person handles outside resources, I'm happy to use their preferred process.

Thank you,
[Name]
[Company, direct contact, website]

The purpose is to learn whether there is a useful fit. Do not insert invented familiarity, claim to share clients, or suggest that the recipient has endorsed you. Keep gifts, fees, and reciprocal promises out of this introduction.

Keep a useful follow-up record

Track the professional relationship separately from any client case. The relationship record should help you remember what was discussed and whether another contact is welcome.

Follow up when you promised, with what the person asked for. If there was no response, one brief, relevant check-in is a reasonable starting approach; then pause unless they invite further contact. Respect a request to stop immediately. Repeated "just checking in" messages add work for the recipient without explaining your usefulness.

Do not put client names, private inventories, health information, or estate documents in this tracker. If an introduction becomes an inquiry, use a separate case ID in your protected client system.

Make the first introduction easy to handle

  1. Let the client choose. Offer a contact route the professional can share. Before receiving or passing along client details, confirm that the client has agreed to the introduction.
  2. Confirm fit directly. An introduction does not establish scope, authorization, or availability. Use the same qualification process you use for other inquiries.
  3. Agree on updates. Ask the client who may receive information and what can be shared. A professional introduction is not blanket permission to disclose the case.
  4. Close the communication loop. Send only the update that was agreed. If the work is unsuitable or your calendar is full, communicate that promptly so the family can decide what to do next.

At your next internal review, ask whether the handoff was clear, the scope matched, and promised updates happened. Use the growth guide's intake funnel to see what happened after the introduction. The aim is a relationship both businesses can understand and maintain, without promising a stream of work.
