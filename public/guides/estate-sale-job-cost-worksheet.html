Is This Estate Sale Worth Taking? A Job-Cost and Minimum-Fee Worksheet

A full calendar does not tell you what each job earns. Put the work, the fee structure, and the downside case on one sheet before quoting.

By Joel PrentissPublished November 8, 2026

In this guide

  1. Start with the work you are agreeing to deliver
  2. Count the entire job, including your own time
  3. Define the money the company actually retains
  4. Test low, central, and high sales
  5. Use the minimum to expose a decision, not conceal a risk
  6. Fill in the worksheet before quoting, then reconcile it

Start with the work you are agreeing to deliver

An estate with promising contents can still be a poor fit for the available crew, preparation window, or required closeout. The useful question is not simply how much the items might sell for. It is how much your business would retain, what delivering the work would cost, and what changes if sales disappoint.

Use this worksheet after qualifying the inquiry and inspecting enough of the property to make a responsible estimate. List the included rooms, retained items, sale format, preparation days, selling days, pickup work, and leftover plan. Mark assumptions that still need confirmation.

This is an original planning model for an owner, not an appraisal, a recommended commission, a quote, or tax advice. The Small Business Administration’s planning guidance provides the general break-even principle of comparing revenue and costs. The figures here are invented to make that comparison inspectable.

Count the entire job, including your own time

Record estimated hours by task and person. Include loading supplies, travel, sorting, research, photography, setup, sale-day work, pickup, reconciliation, and returning the property. Multiply hours by the applicable business cost per hour. Do not treat the owner’s time as free simply because it is not paid through the same payroll run.

Use your actual staffing costs, including applicable payroll costs and coverage. How a worker is classified is a separate question to resolve correctly; calling someone a contractor does not make the planning cost disappear. Keep specialist work, materials, payment processing, travel, and closeout visible rather than hiding them in a single guess.

Choose a consistent overhead allocation, such as a share based on planned productive crew days, and document it. Allocate the same monthly expense once across jobs. If subscriptions or insurance are already in that allocation, do not add the full monthly bill again to each sale.

Processing costs and some labor vary with activity. A fixed allowance is only a simplification for this example. Recalculate those costs in your real low, central, and high scenarios; an unexpected extra setup day can matter more than a small change in the commission rate.

Define the money the company actually retains

For this example, G is item sales after discounts and refunds, excluding sales tax. c is the commission rate, M is a minimum commission, F is a separate agreed service fee retained by the company, and C is the complete planned cost.

Retained fees = the greater of (G × c) or M, plus F.
Planned job result = retained fees − C.

This minimum replaces a smaller percentage commission. It is not added to the percentage. Some agreements work differently, so rewrite the formula to match your actual terms. Identify whether a deposit is a credit toward the final fee, an advance on proceeds, or a different charge. Never count the same money twice.

Here, all listed delivery costs remain in C, even where a service fee helps cover them. Pure pass-through reimbursements are excluded from both revenue and cost in this simplified model. In your records, either include both sides or exclude both consistently. Sales tax collected from customers is kept outside retained fees.

Test low, central, and high sales

Assume a 35% commission, a $3,000 minimum commission, a separate $500 service fee, and $4,200 in total costs. These are hypothetical terms, not typical Texas prices. Costs are held constant here solely to isolate the fee calculation.

The low case loses $700 even though the minimum applies. The central case leaves $500 after the owner-time allowance and allocated overhead, before income taxes and any omitted costs. That result is useful only if the scope and cost assumptions are credible.

Build the range from the available contents, condition, exclusions, selling method, and your relevant completed-job records. Do not substitute original retail prices or unsold online asking prices for likely sale receipts. If a few unverified specialist items determine whether the job works, identify that uncertainty before relying on them.

Use the minimum to expose a decision, not conceal a risk

If the owner wants a $500 planning surplus after $4,200 of costs, retained fees must total $4,700. With the separate $500 service fee, the commission component must supply $4,200. A $4,200 minimum commission would meet that target in this fixed-cost model when it is collectible under the agreed terms.

Without that higher minimum, a 35% commission reaches $4,200 at $12,000 of item sales. This is a calculation from the assumptions, not a recommended acceptance threshold. If costs increase, the threshold changes. If the client cannot pay an applicable shortfall, a number in a worksheet does not create cash.

Now inspect the family’s side. At $8,000 in sales, a $4,200 minimum plus a $500 fee leaves $3,300 before any other agreed deductions. The job may cover your model while failing the family’s needs. Explain the net-proceeds scenario clearly using the proposal-comparison guide.

The available decisions include reducing an optional part of the scope, changing the schedule or method, obtaining specialist information, revising the fee with informed agreement, or declining. Do not present a high minimum as proof that the contents justify a sale.

Fill in the worksheet before quoting, then reconcile it

Download the blank job-cost worksheet (CSV)

The download has low, central, high, and actual columns. It contains prompts and calculation instructions, not automatic spreadsheet formulas. Enter your own hours, rates, fee definitions, cost allowances, and target. Print this page beside it if you want the worked example.

  1. Write the scope and exclusions first, with the assumptions still to verify.
  2. Enter all costs and the basis for each estimate. Check that owner labor, travel, and closeout are present.
  3. Calculate retained fees under each sales scenario using the exact minimum-fee rule.
  4. Review both the company result and the family’s expected net. Record who approved the quote.
  5. After closeout, replace estimates with actual hours, fees, and costs. Record the reason for each material difference.

Keep a short learning note: “two additional setup days because retained items were mixed with sale items” is more useful than “labor over budget.” Update your next estimate from that evidence. The worksheet earns its place by improving the next decision, not by producing a reassuring number for the current one.
