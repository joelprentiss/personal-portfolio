What Should an Estate Sale Payout Statement Include?

A family should be able to follow the money from the sale records to the payment. Agree the reporting detail in advance, then make every deduction and remaining amount traceable.

By Joel PrentissPublished November 8, 2026

In this guide

  2. Make the statement answer eight questions
  3. Follow one illustrative reconciliation
  4. Connect the summary to supporting records
  5. Explain unsettled money and remaining property

Agree the statement before the sale begins

A payment amount by itself does not explain an estate sale. The closing statement should connect the agreed fee terms, the recorded sales, the expenses, and the money already paid. It should also make clear whether the account is final or whether a specific amount remains unsettled.

Ask about the statement while comparing companies, not only after the last shopping day. Agree when it will arrive, what detail it will contain, how payment will be made, and who can answer questions. A redacted sample or a labeled example can reveal differences that a promise of “full reporting” leaves unclear.

This is an original recommended outline, not a claim that every company supplies item-level accounting or that one payout deadline governs every estate sale. The agreement, sale format, and applicable requirements matter. It is also not a substitute for an executor’s probate accounting.

For a firm, choose a report format your actual records can support. If small items are sold in bundles or recorded by category, explain that before the client expects an individual sale record for every object. The commission and fees guide helps establish the calculation terms the statement will later use.

Make the statement answer eight questions

  1. Which job and period does this cover? Include an estate or project identifier, company details, sale dates, statement date, recipient, and version. Avoid putting unnecessary household details in email subject lines.
  2. What was sold and for how much? Show the agreed level of transaction, item, lot, or category detail and the total. Explain whether discounts are already reflected in recorded prices.
  3. What changed the sale total? Identify refunds, corrections, cancellations, and unsettled transactions with references to the supporting records.
  4. How was sales tax handled? Keep the tax amounts separate from item proceeds. Identify the responsible party and distinguish an amount recorded for remittance from evidence that remittance has occurred.
  5. How was the company fee calculated? State the percentage, calculation base, minimum rule, and any separate agreed fee. Show the math rather than only a deduction labeled “commission.”
  6. Which expenses were charged? Itemize each approved seller-paid expense with the date, purpose, amount, receipt or invoice reference, and relevant approval.
  7. What has been paid, and what remains? Reconcile total seller proceeds, previous payments, any specifically agreed unresolved amount, and the payment now due. Distinguish scheduled, sent, and received status.
  8. What happened to the unsold property? Attach the agreed disposition record and identify goods still awaiting a decision, collection, or removal.

Include a contact for corrections and a clear way to cite a line or transaction. A family should not have to reconstruct the entire sale to ask about one hauling charge.

Follow one illustrative reconciliation

This example is invented. Assume $12,800 of item sales before refunds, with discounts already reflected, plus $960 of tax recorded at checkout. Customers later receive $800 of item refunds and $60 of related tax refunds. The tax figures are bookkeeping placeholders, not a determination of any Texas tax rate or exemption.

The agreement in this example charges 35% of item sales after refunds, excluding tax, with no minimum or other service fee. There are $650 of approved expenses and a $3,000 payment already made to the seller. All remaining customer payments have settled; there is no reserve or outstanding dispute.

The $3,000 previous payment reduces the balance due; it does not reduce the seller’s total proceeds to $4,150. The $900 tax line is not a company fee or seller income in this illustration. A report should keep those distinctions readable even when a payment processor combines several transactions in one deposit.

Connect the summary to supporting records

For the operator, build a reconciliation from transaction records to cash counts and processor settlement records. Explain differences such as unsettled card payments, processor fees, refunds, and corrections. A bank deposit after fees is not interchangeable with the gross item-sales figure used in a commission calculation.

Check for duplicate deductions. Discounts already reflected in selling prices should not be subtracted again. Refunds already removed from the commission base should not also reduce seller proceeds twice. A deposit paid toward an agreed company fee should be credited under those terms, not silently treated as an additional charge.

For Texas businesses within its scope, the Comptroller’s recordkeeping guidance requires records supporting receipts and claimed deductions or exclusions. It generally calls for sales and use tax records to be kept at least four years, with longer retention in specified audit or dispute circumstances. That tax guidance does not by itself define every field in a client payout statement.

Share supporting detail at the level agreed with the client while protecting shopper information and payment data. A transaction identifier can connect the report to the underlying record without exposing full card details. Keep an original statement and a dated correction rather than overwriting history without explanation.

Explain unsettled money and remaining property

If a transaction is unresolved, identify its amount, reference, current status, and next review date. Explain how the agreement handles it and which part of the seller balance it affects. A vague “pending” label does not tell the family whether the company is waiting for settlement, handling a dispute, or correcting a record.

Any reserve or withheld amount should be tied to the agreed terms and a specific explanation, with a release or review process. This guide does not authorize withholding money or establish a legal right to delay payment. Obtain appropriate advice if the agreement or entitlement is disputed.

Track unsold goods separately from the money summary. Suggested fields are item or group, agreed destination, authorizing person, date, recipient or vendor, cost, and evidence reference. Label a planned donation or pickup as planned until it happens.

Attach available pickup, donation, or disposal documentation promised in the scope. A receipt showing transfer does not establish a tax deduction or an appraised value. Do not assign leftover goods to staff or a third party without the required authority and agreement.

Use the outline to prepare or review a statement

Download the blank payout outline (CSV)

The download contains sections for identity, calculation, payment status, outstanding items, and property disposition. It is a blank planning and review tool with instructions, not an automatic accounting system. Adapt it to the agreement and the records available.

For a family, start by matching the sale dates, fee basis, expenses, and prior payments against your own records. Mark a specific question beside any unclear line. Request the referenced evidence or an explanation; do not assume that a missing explanation establishes misconduct.

For an operator, have someone check the calculations and attachments before delivery where practical. Confirm the intended recipient through the existing client process. Record when the statement was delivered and when each payment was sent, then update any remaining item to its actual final status.

If you ask for a review at this milestone, use the same optional process for eligible clients regardless of sentiment. Receiving proceeds or raising a statement question should not depend on a favorable review. The review-request guide includes neutral wording and a routing audit.
