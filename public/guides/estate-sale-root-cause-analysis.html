How to Find the Real Cause of Repeated Estate Sale Problems

Record what happened before deciding why. Rank recurring problems, test the proposed explanation, and check whether the corrective action prevents the same failure.

By Joel PrentissPublished November 8, 2026

Separate the observed failure from the explanation

Find the cause of a repeated estate sale problem by defining the failure, collecting comparable observations, grouping them, and testing a proposed explanation against evidence. “Someone was careless” is not a useful root cause unless the work conditions and facts have actually been examined.

Begin with a narrow event, such as “the cashier could not determine the current authorized price from the available record.” That definition is different from an item being difficult to value or a customer asking for a lower price. Mixing those events produces a misleading count.

Record the event close to when it occurs. Preserve relevant references without collecting unnecessary customer details. Include what is known, what is uncertain, and what immediate correction was made so the sale could continue appropriately.

Use frequency to choose an investigation

The following is an invented set of 20 issue tickets, each assigned one primary category. It is not a sample of real clients or a claim about industry frequency. A Pareto review orders categories by frequency so the team can see where investigation may be useful.

Hypothetical issue-ticket Pareto table
Primary categoryTicketsShareCumulative share
Missing readable price/reference840%40%
Outdated markdown instruction525%65%
Unclear group boundary420%85%
Unmatched sold-status record315%100%

The first two categories account for 13 of 20 tickets, or 65%, in this invented set. Do not force an 80/20 pattern onto the data. Frequency also does not override consequence: a rare serious safety or unauthorized-release issue may need attention before the most common inconvenience.

ASQ’s Pareto guidance describes ranking categories by frequency or another stated measure. Keep the unit and observation period consistent. A category with eight tickets is a starting point for investigation, not proof of a single shared cause.

Test each answer in the 5 Whys chain

For the missing-price category, a hypothetical investigation might ask: why was the price unavailable at checkout? Perhaps the label was detached. Why did it detach? Perhaps the chosen label did not remain attached to that packaging. Why was it used? Perhaps the kit instructions did not distinguish that surface or provide an approved alternative.

Every “perhaps” needs evidence. Inspect the relevant goods and labels, compare affected and unaffected examples, review the instruction, and ask the people who did the work. Some of the eight tickets may instead involve an item that was never labeled or a group split after labeling. Those require different explanations.

ASQ’s Five Whys guidance notes that the investigation may take fewer or more than five questions. Do not invent a fifth answer to complete a worksheet. Stop claiming certainty where the evidence stops, and branch the investigation when there are multiple plausible paths.

A useful cause statement identifies a condition the process can change and the evidence connecting it to the failure. “Staff need to care more” provides neither a test nor a dependable corrective action.

Separate correction, prevention, and verification

Replacing a missing label corrects the immediate problem. Testing an appropriate identification method and updating the kit instruction may address one demonstrated cause. Checking the next comparable work period tells you whether that action worked and whether it created another problem.

Name the action owner, due date, test conditions, expected observation, and stop rule. If the hypothesis predicts that a specific surface causes detachment, test that prediction under suitable conditions before changing every label in the business. Do not experiment on client goods in a way that risks damage.

These checks are temporary and are not saved. Print the page or record findings in your own working log.

Check for recurrence without overstating the result

If no issue appears in one small trial, say exactly what was observed and what was tested. That is not proof the problem has been eliminated. Continue the defined check over suitable comparable work and record changes in scope or conditions.

If the issue returns, inspect whether the action was used, whether the original cause was incomplete, or whether a different failure now produces the same symptom. Do not automatically blame the person who discovered the recurrence.

The pilot and control-plan guide helps carry the investigation into daily work. Link the revised instruction to the training record so the correction is taught and observed rather than remaining in an owner’s notes.
