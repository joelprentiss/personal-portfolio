Estate Sale Commissions and Extra Fees: Compare What You Actually Keep

A percentage is one part of the proposal. Put the included work, minimum charges, extra costs, and expected seller proceeds beside one another before deciding.

By Joel PrentissPublished November 8, 2026

In this guide

  1. Compare the whole proposed job
  2. Ask what each number means
  3. Use the same sales assumption for both proposals
  4. Fill in a comparison sheet with the firm
  5. Check what happens beyond the arithmetic
  6. Choose with the unresolved questions answered

Compare the whole proposed job

Two estate sale companies can quote different percentages while offering different services. One may include preparation and removal of remaining goods; another may bill those separately. A lower percentage can leave a family with less money once a minimum or additional charges apply.

EstateSales.NET’s consumer guide also cautions against choosing by percentage alone. That does not mean the highest commission is best. It means the percentage is incomplete information. Ask each firm to explain its own proposal against the same description of the contents and work.

This guide is for families comparing liquidation proposals, including households around Georgetown, Round Rock, Cedar Park, and Austin. All example prices are invented for arithmetic. They are not local averages, standard rates, quotes, or promises about what your property will earn.

First, write down your goal: sell suitable contents, clear agreed spaces by a deadline, preserve certain belongings, or arrange another service. If you need a personal-property appraisal for a specific purpose, ask who performs that assignment and what it includes. Sale pricing and an appraisal report are different services.

Ask what each number means

  • Commission basis: Which receipts receive the percentage? Ask how discounts, refunds, sales tax, specialist sales, and goods sold through another channel are treated.
  • Minimum: Is it the lowest total commission, a minimum total fee, or a separate charge? Does it replace a smaller percentage charge or add to it? What happens if the proceeds do not cover the amount due?
  • Setup and labor: What work and how many days are included? Ask what triggers an additional charge and how you approve it.
  • Other expenses: Identify advertising, supplies, specialist work, travel, payment processing, cleaning, hauling, and disposal where relevant. Ask whether an estimate, cap, or actual receipt governs each one.
  • Deposits and adjustments: Confirm whether money paid before the sale is credited against the final invoice. Ask who bears refunds, disputed payments, cancellations, and changes to the retained-item list.

Record “not yet answered” when a proposal is silent. An empty box does not mean a service is free or included. Ask for written clarification before comparing totals or signing.

The American Society of Estate Liquidators’ consumer questions cover included services, extra charges, cleanup, and records. Use those questions to start a conversation, then document the specific answers from the firm you are considering.

Use the same sales assumption for both proposals

For the following illustration, gross item proceeds means receipts after discounts and refunds, excluding sales tax. Both companies are assumed to sell the same contents for the same amount. That isolates fees; it does not predict that their actual selling results would be identical.

Illustrative proposal A: 30% commission, with a $3,500 minimum commission, plus $1,200 setup and $400 closeout.
Illustrative proposal B: 40% commission, with a $4,200 minimum commission, including the same setup and closeout work. No other seller-paid charges in either example.

Each minimum replaces a smaller percentage commission. For example, proposal A at $12,000 in item proceeds charges the greater of $3,600 or $3,500, then adds $1,600. Its total charge is $5,200, leaving $6,800. Proposal B charges $4,800, leaving $7,200.

In this example, B leaves more at the lower two sales levels, while A leaves more at $20,000. There is no winner at every level. The minimums matter most when sales are low, and the percentage difference matters more as sales rise.

Ask each firm what supports its estimated range and what it has inspected. An optimistic sales estimate can make a fee look harmless. Keep a shared low scenario in the comparison even if one proposal forecasts much higher proceeds.

Fill in a comparison sheet with the firm

Download the blank proposal comparison (CSV)

The worksheet has columns for two proposals and the basis or question behind each line. It provides calculation instructions without automatic formulas. Use the same low, central, and high item-proceeds figures for both proposals, then keep each firm’s own forecast separately labeled.

  1. Enter the percentage and the exact minimum rule. Identify whether the fee base is already adjusted for refunds.
  2. List every additional seller-paid charge, its trigger, and whether it is fixed, capped, estimated, or based on actual costs.
  3. Calculate the commission once, add the separate charges once, and subtract the total from the comparable proceeds.
  4. Account for deposits already paid so they do not become an extra fee by accident.
  5. Mark unknown charges as unresolved. Request a revised written illustration before treating the net as comparable.

If your starting proceeds figure already excludes refunded sales, do not subtract the same refund again. If a processor deposits a net amount after taking its fees, do not treat that deposit as gross sales and then deduct those processing fees a second time.

Keep tax collected from buyers separately identified. This worksheet does not decide the tax treatment or rate for your transaction. Have the operator explain its method and resolve transaction-specific questions with the appropriate tax professional or authority.

Check what happens beyond the arithmetic

A comparable total requires comparable work. Ask who supervises preparation and sale days, how excluded belongings are marked, how discounts are authorized, what records you receive, and how remaining items are handled. If a service is absent from one proposal, either add a realistic separately quoted cost or acknowledge the difference.

Be precise about “cleanout.” Does it mean removing specified unsold goods, arranging a third-party pickup, or delivering every room empty? Who pays if a donation provider declines an item? Where will retained belongings remain? A vague word should not carry a costly assumption.

Confirm the payout schedule and how unsettled payments will be reported. Ask to see a redacted sample or a clearly labeled example statement. The payout-statement guide explains how sale totals, charges, prior payments, and remaining balances can be made understandable.

Check references and any credential or insurance claim with the relevant source. Distinguish seller-client experience from shopping reviews. A strong shopping experience can tell you something about sale-day service, but it does not show how the firm handled another family’s accounting and closeout.

Choose with the unresolved questions answered

Before deciding, read the actual written agreement alongside the comparison. Identify every difference between what was said, what was estimated, and what is promised. A worksheet helps you ask questions; it does not replace the agreement or determine your legal rights.

If the low scenario leaves too little for the family or creates an unaffordable fee shortfall, discuss alternatives before signing. A different scope, sale method, or service may fit better. An owner can use the job-cost worksheet to explain why a particular scope requires a particular fee, but the family still needs to decide whether the proposal meets its needs.

Save the dated proposal, clarified fee example, agreed exclusions, and later approved changes together. Those records give both sides a common starting point when the final statement arrives.
