Estate Sale KPIs: What to Measure Beyond Gross Sales

Choose measures that explain the work and the promises being kept. Give every rate a clear numerator, denominator, time window, and decision owner.

By Joel PrentissPublished November 8, 2026

  2. Use a scorecard with explicit denominators

Measure work quality and delivery alongside sales

Useful estate sale KPIs show how much work a job required, what had to be corrected, whether the sale was ready, and whether the agreed closeout happened on time. Gross sales alone do not reveal those conditions. Start with a small scorecard that the team can collect reliably and use to make a decision.

Write the definition before collecting a number. “Errors” could mean incorrect transactions, individual defects, or customer questions. “Cleared” could mean sold, physically removed, or transferred through an agreed disposition. Those measures are not interchangeable.

Use operational data to improve the process, not to rank people across unlike assignments. A staff member assigned difficult research or unresolved client decisions may spend more time while preventing a serious mistake.

If a denominator is zero, show “not applicable” rather than a perfect rate. If data are missing, label them missing rather than entering zero. Keep the count behind every percentage visible, particularly when the sample is small.

Distinguish defects from affected transactions

In a hypothetical audit, 40 transactions are reviewed and two contain at least one defined pricing error. One of those transactions contains two separate errors. The affected-transaction rate is 2 ÷ 40 = 5% in the audited set. There are three individual defects, but using three as the numerator would answer a different question.

This small example does not estimate a stable business-wide rate or a Six Sigma performance level. If the audit deliberately selected suspicious transactions, say so. A targeted check is useful for finding problems but should not be represented as a random sample of all work.

Use the pricing-error guide to define the error and the root-cause guide to investigate it. The scorecard identifies a question; it does not establish the cause.

Make collection part of the existing handoff

Collect setup hours from the same task record used for planning, readiness from the release check, and pickup from the release record. Avoid making staff retype the same event into several unrelated spreadsheets. The inventory guide explains how shared references support those connections.

Assign a definition owner and a review time for each measure. Include a version date. If the definition changes, mark the break in the series rather than comparing the new number directly with the old one.

ASQ’s check-sheet guidance supports defining the observed event and trialing the collection form. Test whether two people classify the same sample consistently before relying on the resulting count.

Compare relevant work and keep context visible

Record scope, sale format, staff mix, access constraints, and material changes beside each job’s measures. Ten hours spent on one room with many research exceptions cannot be meaningfully ranked against ten hours spent on a different kind of work without considering the difference.

Show individual observations and counts before summarizing them. A small set of heterogeneous sales does not justify precise claims about process capability, statistical control, or causation. Ask for appropriate analytical expertise before using advanced methods that require stronger assumptions and data.

Pair speed with quality and client commitments. If setup hours fall but relabeling at checkout rises, investigate the tradeoff. If a high clearance figure hides goods transferred without the necessary approval, the measure is rewarding the wrong outcome.

Give the scorecard a decision, not just a display

These checks are temporary and are not saved. Print the page or record findings in your own working log.

Before adopting a KPI

Review a few measures at a regular planning point. Choose one problem to investigate rather than launching a project for every movement in the scorecard. Connect the financial question to the existing job-cost worksheet, which includes owner time and overhead.

Retire a measure that nobody uses only after confirming it is not needed for an agreed record or obligation. A useful scorecard helps the team make a better next decision. It does not need to make every week look like an improvement.
